I have never really thought about a place's water as much as I have in my time in the San Luis Valley. Every livable place needs it. Yet here it is closer to a livelihood than a resource. Nearly a third of the Valley's economic base is accounted for by agriculture that depends on a high-alpine aquifer being slowly drained. That reality is what sent me east, to Kansas, where a decade-old experiment in voluntary water governance has now accumulated enough data to say something real.

The Kansas Local Enhanced Management Area (LEMA) program gives Groundwater Management Districts the authority to write their own conservation plans, letting farmers set their own pumping caps rather than wait for state-imposed cuts. Sheridan County farmers were the first to act on that authority in 2013, agreeing to hold each irrigator to a five-year allocation of 55 acre-inches. Three more LEMAs followed across northwestern Kansas. Together they form a natural staggered experiment: four adoption cohorts, fourteen counties, and years of outcome data now available from public sources.

"The water table was falling about 0.38 feet per year before these restrictions. Post-adoption, that rate fell to 0.15 feet per year, roughly a 60 percent slowdown in the rate of aquifer decline."

The Four LEMA Cohorts

Each LEMA was established through a signed Chief Engineer order from the Kansas Division of Water Resources. The four adoption events used as treatment dates in this analysis are:

Understanding a Water Cut

Limiting groundwater pumping doesn't happen at the press of a button. It goes through a legal process established by Kansas's 2012 Groundwater Management District Act, which allows GMDs to propose plans to be approved by the Kansas Chief Engineer. Once adopted, the district board, in the case of the GMD4 LEMA, the Northwest Kansas Groundwater Management District — notifies every water right holder within 30 days, spelling out each right's five-year allocation.

For the farmers involved, that means treating water like a budget rather than a tap. A fixed allocation may redirect historically corn-heavy acres toward less water-intensive crops like grain sorghum, which demands noticeably less irrigation. These are choices made by individual operators trying to live within a shared number. The multiplier implications run wide: less irrigation means less seed, fertilizer, and chemical inputs bought from local ag retailers. In Sheridan County, one early study estimated seed and chemical spending fell by roughly 20 percent in the years after restriction took effect.

Variable 1 — Groundwater

The most important variable and the most useful in the data. Rather than comparing average water levels, which conflates secular aquifer decline with any treatment effect, this analysis estimates the slope (feet per year) of water table decline for each county's monitoring wells in the ten years before and ten years after each LEMA adoption, then compares those slopes.

Across eleven counties with sufficient well data, the average decline rate fell from 0.38 ft/yr before adoption to 0.15 ft/yr after, a 60 percent slowdown. The sharpest individual result was Sheridan County itself: from 0.63 ft/yr pre-adoption to 0.08 ft/yr after. Nearly a steady water table after more than a decade of restrictions. Wichita County, a more recent adopter (2021), moved from 0.29 to 0.06 ft/yr.

Not every county fits the pattern. Sherman, Thomas, and Wallace are still losing groundwater each year, just somewhat slower. Graham, Logan, and Cheyenne actually show slightly faster decline post-adoption. These are changes of a few hundredths of a foot per year, small enough to be driven by weather variation rather than policy reversal. Lane, Scott, and Greeley were excluded from the groundwater comparison entirely: monitoring well counts in those counties dropped so sharply after their 2023 adoption that estimating a meaningful trend wasn't possible.

The TWFE regression. Controls for county fixed effects and adoption cohort fixed effects, estimates that LEMA adoption is associated with a 0.14 ft/yr reduction in the groundwater decline rate (p ≈ 0.10). With only 22 observations (11 counties × 2 periods), that result should be treated as directionally consistent with the pre/post comparison rather than a precise causal estimate.

Variable 2 — Crop Mix

One response to a water budget is to grow less thirsty crops. If restrictions were driving real behavioral change, we'd expect to see cropland shift away from corn, which requires heavy irrigation, toward grain sorghum, which can be grown on significantly less water. Sorghum's share of total tracked cropland across LEMA counties rose from about 10.3 percent before adoption to 14.5 percent after, a 41 percent relative increase.

That finding comes with a real data caveat. USDA's NASS suppresses county-level crop acreage when too few farms are reporting, to protect farm privacy. The sorghum numbers are built on a thinner set of county-year observations than the groundwater or income data, skewed toward the years and counties where enough farms reported to publish. The crop mix shift is consistent with the expected behavioral response to water restrictions, but should be read as suggestive rather than definitive.

Variable 3 — Farm Income

Average farm proprietors' income across the fourteen counties ran about $30,700 per year before LEMA adoption and $28,400 afterward, a modest decline. In the TWFE event-study specification, however, none of the year-by-year income coefficients reached statistical significance. The variation in farm income across this panel is dominated by commodity price cycles and drought years, not the policy event.

This is also where the sub-county boundary limitation bites hardest. County-level BEA income figures blend farms inside the LEMA boundary with farms entirely outside it. If restriction reduced income for farms actually subject to the cap, that effect would be diluted across the full county total. The null farm income result is consistent with either a true null effect, or a real effect that the available data simply cannot resolve at county scale.

safe_pull <- function(short_desc, label, years = 2000:2025,
                       county_filter = TRUE, agg_level = "COUNTY") {
  cat("Attempting:", label, "...\n")
  params <- list(
    source_desc   = "SURVEY",
    state_alpha   = "KS",
    short_desc    = short_desc,
    year          = years,
    agg_level_desc = agg_level
  )
  if (county_filter) params$county_name <- counties
  result <- tryCatch(nassqs(params), error = function(e) {
    cat("  FAILED:", conditionMessage(e), "\n")
    NULL
  })
  if (!is.null(result)) cat("  SUCCESS:", nrow(result), "rows returned.\n")
  result
}

safe_pull_line <- function(table_name, line_code, label) {
  cat("Attempting:", label, "...\n")
  tryCatch(
    beaGet(list(
      UserID      = beaKey,
      Method      = "GetData",
      DatasetName = "Regional",
      TableName   = table_name,
      LineCode    = line_code,
      GeoFips     = paste(county_fips, collapse = ","),
      Year        = "ALL",
      ResultFormat = "json"
    )),
    error = function(e) { cat("  FAILED:", conditionMessage(e), "\n"); NULL }
  )
}
 
# CAINC1: requires per-county loop when LineCode = ALL
pull_cainc1_looped <- function() {
  results <- lapply(county_fips, function(f) {
    tryCatch(
      beaGet(list(
        UserID = beaKey, Method = "GetData", DatasetName = "Regional",
        TableName = "CAINC1", LineCode = "ALL", GeoFips = f,
        Year = "ALL", ResultFormat = "json"
      )),
      error = function(e) NULL
    )
  })
  bind_rows(results[!sapply(results, is.null)])
}

# 05_analysis.R
#
# Merges groundwater slopes, NASS, and BEA into one county-year panel
# aligned on each county's first LEMA adoption year.
#
# METHODS NOTE: an earlier version compared each well's residual from its
# all-time mean depth. That approach is wrong for testing "did LEMA slow
# the decline" -- because the aquifer has been declining continuously,
# any early year looks below average and any late year above average almost
# by construction. The correct test is to compare the SLOPE (ft/yr) pre vs
# post LEMA, not the average level.
 
library(dplyr)
library(tidyr)
library(readr)
library(ggplot2)
library(fixest)
 
clean_county <- function(x) x |> toupper() |> trimws() |> gsub(" COUNTY$", "", x = _)
 
lema_timeline <- readRDS("data_clean/lema_timeline.rds") %>%
  mutate(county_key = clean_county(county))
 
county_treatment <- lema_timeline %>%
  group_by(county_key) %>%
  summarise(
    first_lema           = lema[which.min(effective_start)],
    first_treatment_year = min(effective_start),
    n_lemas              = n_distinct(lema),
    .groups = "drop"
  )
 
# --- Groundwater: well-level slope comparison ---
gw_raw <- read_csv("data_raw/kgs_gwl_clean.csv", show_col_types = FALSE)
 
get_well_slopes <- function(county_name_key, anchor_year, window = 10, min_n = 3) {
  gw_raw %>%
    filter(valid_reading, clean_county(county) == county_name_key) %>%
    mutate(period = case_when(
      year >= anchor_year - window & year < anchor_year ~ "pre",
      year >= anchor_year & year <= anchor_year + window ~ "post",
      TRUE ~ NA_character_
    )) %>%
    filter(!is.na(period)) %>%
    group_by(period, USGS_ID) %>%
    filter(n() >= min_n) %>%
    summarise(slope = coef(lm(DEPTH_TO_WATER ~ year))[2], .groups = "drop") %>%
    group_by(period) %>%
    summarise(avg_slope_ft_per_yr = mean(slope, na.rm = TRUE),
              n_wells = n(), .groups = "drop") %>%
    mutate(county_key = county_name_key)
}
 
gw_slopes_by_county <- bind_rows(lapply(seq_len(nrow(county_treatment)), function(i) {
  get_well_slopes(county_treatment$county_key[i], county_treatment$first_treatment_year[i])
}))
 
# --- TWFE: groundwater (Lane, Scott, Greeley excluded -- well attrition) ---
gw_reg <- gw_slopes_by_county %>%
  mutate(post_indicator = ifelse(period == "post", 1, 0)) %>%
  left_join(county_treatment %>% select(county_key, first_treatment_year),
            by = "county_key") %>%
  filter(!county_key %in% c("LANE", "SCOTT", "GREELEY"))
 
twfe_gw <- feols(avg_slope_ft_per_yr ~ post_indicator | county_key + first_treatment_year,
                 data = gw_reg, vcov = "hetero")
etable(twfe_gw)
 
# --- TWFE event-study: farm proprietors income ---
master_panel <- county_treatment %>%
  left_join(crop_panel, by = "county_key") %>%
  left_join(bea_panel,  by = c("county_key", "year")) %>%
  filter(!is.na(year)) %>%
  mutate(
    event_time    = year - first_treatment_year,
    sorghum_share = sorghum / (corn_total + wheat_winter + sorghum + soybeans)
  )
 
twfe_income <- feols(
  farm_proprietors_income ~ i(event_time, ref = -1) | county_key + year,
  data  = master_panel %>% filter(event_time >= -6, event_time <= 6),
  vcov  = "hetero"
)
etable(twfe_income)
 
write_csv(master_panel,         "output/master_panel.csv")
write_csv(gw_slopes_by_county,  "output/groundwater_slopes_by_county.csv")

Methodology

Design, data sources, and limitations.

Data Sources

  • KGS WIZARD — well-level groundwater levels (manual download, all 14 counties)
  • USDA NASS QuickStats — crop acreage by county (rnassqs API)
  • BEA CAINC5N, Line 71 — farm proprietors' income (bea.R API)
  • BEA CAINC1 — personal income and population

Identification Strategy

  • Staggered DiD: four adoption cohorts (2013, 2018, 2021, 2023)
  • Event-time window: ±10 years for groundwater; ±6 for income
  • Groundwater: well-level OLS slope (ft/yr) pre vs post, then county average
  • TWFE with county and cohort/year fixed effects; heteroskedasticity-robust SE

Critical Limitations

  • LEMA boundaries are sub-county; county-level data dilutes the treatment signal
  • Wallace and Sheridan each fall under two overlapping LEMAs
  • Lane, Scott, Greeley excluded from groundwater comparison (well attrition)
  • NASS irrigated corn breakout unavailable from 2019 onward; sorghum suppressed in small-farm counties
  • Farm income null result may reflect data limitation, not true null effect

Framing

  • Results framed as "patterns consistent with LEMA adoption" — not precise causal estimates
  • Commodity price cycles and drought confound income comparisons
  • All data sources free and publicly available; no NRD/GMD outreach required
  • Full R pipeline available above; API keys stored in .Renviron, never hardcoded
