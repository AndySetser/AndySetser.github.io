I have never really thought about a place's water as much as I have in my time in the San Luis Valley. It is the area's most fragile resource. Nearly a third of the Valley's economic base is accounted for by agriculture that depends on a high-alpine aquifer being slowly drained. That reality is what led me to study the economics of water regulations, my study will focus on my homestate of Kansas, where decent public data exist around a decade-old experiment in voluntary water governance.

The Kansas Local Enhanced Management Area (LEMA) program gives Groundwater Management Districts the authority to write their own water conservation plans, letting farmers set their own pumping caps rather than wait for state-imposed cuts. Sheridan County farmers were the first to act on that authority in 2013, agreeing to hold each irrigator to a five-year allocation of 55 acre-inches. Three more LEMAs followed across northwestern Kansas. Together I can use them to form a staggered experiment with four adoption cohorts, fourteen counties, and later years of outcome data now available from public sources.

Why Water Cuts exist

Limiting groundwater goes through a legal process established by Kansas's 2012 Groundwater Management District Act, which allows GMDs to propose plans to be approved by the Kansas Chief Engineer. Once adopted, the district board, in the case of the GMD4 LEMA, the Northwest Kansas Groundwater Management District, notifies every water right holder within 30 days, explaining each right's five-year allocation.

A fixed allocation may redirect historically corn-heavy acres toward less water-intensive crops like grain sorghum, which takes far less irrigation. These are choices made by individual operators trying to live within a shared regulation. The multiplier implications runs through these communities, as less irrigation means less seed, fertilizer, and chemical inputs bought from local ag retailers. In Sheridan County, one early study estimated seed and chemical spending fell by roughly 20 percent in the years after restriction took effect.

Variable 1 Groundwater

The most important variable and most useful in the data. This analysis estimates the slope (feet per year) of water table decline for each county's monitoring wells in the ten years before and ten years after each LEMA adoption, then compares those slopes.

Across eleven counties with sufficient well data, the average decline rate fell from 0.38 ft/yr before adoption to 0.15 ft/yr after, a 60 percent slowdown. The sharpest individual result was Sheridan County, going from 0.63 ft/yr pre-adoption to 0.08 ft/yr after. Almost achieving a steady water table after more than a decade of restrictions. Wichita County, a more recent adopter (2021), moved from 0.29 to 0.06 ft/yr.

Not every county fits the pattern though. Sherman, Thomas, and Wallace are still losing groundwater each year, just somewhat slower. Graham, Logan, and Cheyenne actually show slightly faster decline post-adoption. These are changes of a few hundredths of a foot per year, small enough to be driven by weather variation rather than policy reversal. Lane, Scott, and Greeley were excluded from the groundwater comparison entirely because monitoring well counts in those counties dropped so much after their 2023 adoption that I could not form a meaningful trend.

The Two-way fixed effects regression, controlling for county fixed effects and adoption cohort fixed effects, estimated that LEMA adoption is associated with a 0.14 ft/yr reduction in the groundwater decline rate (p ≈ 0.10). However, With the data, I only had 22 observations (11 counties × 2 periods), therefore, that result should be treated as just consistent with the pre/post comparison rather than a precise estimate.

Variable 2 Crop Mix

The first response to a water budget is to grow less water demanding crops. If restrictions were driving real behavioral change, we'd expect to see cropland shift away from corn, which requires heavy irrigation, toward grain sorghum, which can be grown on significantly less water. Sorghum's share of total tracked cropland across LEMA counties rose from about 10.3 percent before adoption to 14.5 percent after, a 41 percent relative increase.

That finding again though has data limitations. USDA's NASS suppresses county-level crop acreage when too few farms are reporting, to protect farm privacy. Studying rural communities, the sorghum numbers are built on a thinner set of county-year observations than the groundwater or income data, skewed toward the years and counties where enough farms reported to publish. The crop mix shift though is what we'd expect in response to water restrictions, but should again only be read as suggestive until more data can be used.

Variable 3 Farm Income

Average farm proprietors' income across the fourteen counties was at about $30,700 per year before LEMA adoption and $28,400 afterward, showing a slight drop. In the Two way fixed effects event-study specification, however, none of the year-by-year income coefficients reached statistical significant levels. The variation in farm income across this panel is most likely impacted greater by commodity price cycles and years of drought, not the Lema adoptions.

This is also where the sub-county boundary limitation fall in. County-level BEA income figures blend farms inside the LEMA boundary with farms entirely outside it. If restriction reduced income for farms actually subject to the cap, that effect would be diluted across the full county total. Another limitation that I ran into with sourcing free and publically sourced data.

safe_pull <- function(short_desc, label, years = 2000:2025,
                       county_filter = TRUE, agg_level = "COUNTY") {
  cat("Attempting:", label, "...\n")
  params <- list(
    source_desc   = "SURVEY",
    state_alpha   = "KS",
    short_desc    = short_desc,
    year          = years,
    agg_level_desc = agg_level
  )
  if (county_filter) params$county_name <- counties
  result <- tryCatch(nassqs(params), error = function(e) {
    cat("  FAILED:", conditionMessage(e), "\n")
    NULL
  })
  if (!is.null(result)) cat("  SUCCESS:", nrow(result), "rows returned.\n")
  result
}

safe_pull_line <- function(table_name, line_code, label) {
  cat("Attempting:", label, "...\n")
  tryCatch(
    beaGet(list(
      UserID      = beaKey,
      Method      = "GetData",
      DatasetName = "Regional",
      TableName   = table_name,
      LineCode    = line_code,
      GeoFips     = paste(county_fips, collapse = ","),
      Year        = "ALL",
      ResultFormat = "json"
    )),
    error = function(e) { cat("  FAILED:", conditionMessage(e), "\n"); NULL }
  )
}


pull_cainc1_looped <- function() {
  results <- lapply(county_fips, function(f) {
    tryCatch(
      beaGet(list(
        UserID = beaKey, Method = "GetData", DatasetName = "Regional",
        TableName = "CAINC1", LineCode = "ALL", GeoFips = f,
        Year = "ALL", ResultFormat = "json"
      )),
      error = function(e) NULL
    )
  })
  bind_rows(results[!sapply(results, is.null)])
}

clean_county <- function(x) x |> toupper() |> trimws() |> gsub(" COUNTY$", "", x = _)

lema_timeline <- readRDS("data_clean/lema_timeline.rds") %>%
  mutate(county_key = clean_county(county))

county_treatment <- lema_timeline %>%
  group_by(county_key) %>%
  summarise(
    first_lema           = lema[which.min(effective_start)],
    first_treatment_year = min(effective_start),
    n_lemas              = n_distinct(lema),
    .groups = "drop"
  )


gw_raw <- read_csv("data_raw/kgs_gwl_clean.csv", show_col_types = FALSE)

get_well_slopes <- function(county_name_key, anchor_year, window = 10, min_n = 3) {
  gw_raw %>%
    filter(valid_reading, clean_county(county) == county_name_key) %>%
    mutate(period = case_when(
      year >= anchor_year - window & year < anchor_year ~ "pre",
      year >= anchor_year & year <= anchor_year + window ~ "post",
      TRUE ~ NA_character_
    )) %>%
    filter(!is.na(period)) %>%
    group_by(period, USGS_ID) %>%
    filter(n() >= min_n) %>%
    summarise(slope = coef(lm(DEPTH_TO_WATER ~ year))[2], .groups = "drop") %>%
    group_by(period) %>%
    summarise(avg_slope_ft_per_yr = mean(slope, na.rm = TRUE),
              n_wells = n(), .groups = "drop") %>%
    mutate(county_key = county_name_key)
}

gw_slopes_by_county <- bind_rows(lapply(seq_len(nrow(county_treatment)), function(i) {
  get_well_slopes(county_treatment$county_key[i], county_treatment$first_treatment_year[i])
}))


gw_reg <- gw_slopes_by_county %>%
  mutate(post_indicator = ifelse(period == "post", 1, 0)) %>%
  left_join(county_treatment %>% select(county_key, first_treatment_year),
            by = "county_key") %>%
  filter(!county_key %in% c("LANE", "SCOTT", "GREELEY"))

twfe_gw <- feols(avg_slope_ft_per_yr ~ post_indicator | county_key + first_treatment_year,
                 data = gw_reg, vcov = "hetero")
etable(twfe_gw)


master_panel <- county_treatment %>%

Methodology

Data Sources

  • KGS WIZARD, well-level groundwater levels (manual download, all 14 counties)
  • USDA NASS QuickStats, crop acreage by county (rnassqs API)
  • BEA CAINC5N, farm proprietors' income (bea.R API)
  • BEA CAINC1, personal income and population

Strategy

  • Staggered DiD: four adoption cohorts (2013, 2018, 2021, 2023)
  • Event-time window: ±10 years for groundwater; ±6 for income
  • Groundwater: well-level OLS slope (ft/yr) pre vs post, then county average
  • TwoWayFixedEffects with county and cohort/year fixed effects

Limitations

  • LEMA boundaries are sub-county; county-level data is not and has to be filtered
  • Wallace and Sheridan each fall under two overlapping LEMAs
  • Lane, Scott, Greeley excluded from groundwater comparison (well attrition)
  • NASS irrigated corn is unavailable from 2019 onward; sorghum data is hidden in small-farm counties
  • Farm income result likely reflect data limitation, not for certain null effect

How to interpret

  • Should be framed as "patterns consistent with LEMA adoption," not precise estimates
  • Commodity price cycles and drought outweigh income comparisons
  • All data sources free and publicly available; no NRD/GMD outreach occurred
  • Full R code above; API keys stored in .Renviron,not displayed
