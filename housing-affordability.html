Between 1985 and 2024, median U.S. home prices rose by nearly 400 percent in nominal terms. Once adjusted for four decades of inflation, that real increase comes to about 71 percent, but still about twice as large as the growth rate of real median household income (36 percent) over the same period. That housing affordability gap, is the finding that this research is focused on.

The hypothesis of this project is that housing prices have disconnected from income growth because of concerning forces, such as insufficient housing supply, sustained monetary accommodation, and demographic demand pressures. This analysis constructs a set of indexed metrics, all on 2024-dollar basis, to test that claim across four decades of data.

Key Findings

1. Price-to-Income separation

The price-to-income ratio, calculated on a consistent real (2024-dollar) basis for both price and income, rose from 4.0 in 1985 to a peak of 5.84 in 2022, before settling at 5.00 in 2024. That's roughly a 25 percent increase in the multiple of income required to buy a median home. When comparing nominal home prices directly against inflation-adjusted income the separation becomes greater. The post-2022 correction, driven by rate pressure suppressing nominal demand, has not meaningfully restored pre-2000 affordability conditions.

2. Inflation Explains some of the gap

A common rebuttal to housing affordability data is that nominal price growth overstates the problem. A claim that inflation-adjusted (real) prices tell a different story. This is partly true, nominal home prices rose 397 percent since 1985, while real prices, restated in constant 2024 dollars, rose about 71 percent. Real household income grew about 36 percent over the same window. Homes did become more expensive in real terms, and by a wider margin than income

3. The 2020–2022 Acceleration Was Unprecedented

While the 2008 housing crisis produced correction, the 2020–2022 pandemic-era price surge stands out for its rapid growth. Nominal prices rose roughly 32 percent in two years (2020–2022), as real income declined slightly. The rate pressure index spiked in 2023, with mortgage rates climbing above 6.5 percent, showing that the market was facing supply-driven price floors and demand-suppressing borrowing costs, a painful combination for buyers.

4. Low Rates disguised Affordability Deterioration (2012–2021)

If viewed through nominal mortgage payments only, the decade from 2012 to 2021 can be misleading on affordability. Low interest rates (below 4 percent for much of this period) held monthly payment costs in check even as home prices grew. This hid the breakdown in the price-to-income relationship. The rate pick up then in 2022–2023 exposed the true affordability problem.

Methodology

Data Sources

  • U.S. Census Bureau, Median Home Sale Price (nominal)
  • U.S. Census Bureau / FRED, Real Median Household Income (2024$)
  • BLS CPI-U, Consumer Price Index (All Urban)
  • FRED, 30-Year Fixed Mortgage Rate

Transformations Applied

  • All series annualized to calendar year
  • Base-year indexing (1985 = 100) for comparability
  • CPI deflation of home price to 2024 dollars, matching the income series basis
  • Log transformation for growth rate analysis

Constructed Metrics

  • Price-to-Income Ratio (P/I), real price ÷ real income
  • Real Home Price Index (CPI-adjusted to 2024$)
  • Affordability Gap Index (Real Home Price Index ÷ Income Index × 100)
  • Rate Pressure Index (Mortgage Rate × real P/I Ratio)

Limitations & Notes

  • Median income series is Census's real (inflation-adjusted, 2024$) median household income (FRED MEHOINUSA672N); home price was rebased to the same real basis for comparison
  • Median home price reflects transactions
  • Income measure is pre-tax household median
  • Geographic variation not captured in national series
  • 2025 row excluded due to incomplete annual data
