Between 1985 and 2024, median U.S. home prices rose by nearly 400 percent. Median household income rose by 36 percent. That gap is persistently embedded in the finding of this analysis.

The core hypothesis of this project is that housing prices have decoupled from income growth not because of a temporary cyclical shock, but because of structural forces: insufficient housing supply, sustained monetary accommodation, and demographic demand pressures. This analysis constructs a set of indexed metrics to test that claim across four decades of data.

"The affordability gap index reached 365.6 in 2024, meaning housing costs have diverged from income by more than three and a half times relative to 1985 baseline conditions, even after adjusting for inflation."

Author's index, 1985 = 100

Key Findings

1. Price-to-Income Divergence Is Structural

The price-to-income ratio rose from 1.37 in 1985 to a peak of 5.45 in 2022, before settling at 5.00 in 2024. This near-quadrupling of the ratio represents a fundamental change in the relationship between earning and purchasing power in housing markets. Even the modest post-2022 correction, driven by rate pressure suppressing nominal demand, has not meaningfully restored pre-2000 affordability conditions.

2. Inflation Explains Part, Not All, of the Gap

A common rebuttal to housing affordability data is that nominal price growth overstates the problem, that inflation-adjusted (real) prices tell a different story. This analysis shows that claim does not hold. The real home price index, CPI-adjusted to 1985 dollars, still rose 70 percent by 2024. Homes have become more expensive in real terms, not just in nominal terms.

3. The 2020–2022 Acceleration Was Unprecedented

While the 2008 housing crisis produced a notable correction, the 2020–2022 pandemic-era price surge stands out for its speed and breadth. Prices rose 31 percent in two years (2020–2022), even as income declined slightly. The rate pressure index spiked in 2023 as mortgage rates climbed above 6.5 percent, revealing a market simultaneously facing supply-driven price floors and demand-suppressing credit costs, an unusual and painful combination for first-time buyers.

4. Low Rates Masked Affordability Deterioration (2012–2021)

The decade from 2012 to 2021 presents a misleading picture if viewed through nominal mortgage payments alone. Ultra-low interest rates (below 4 percent for much of this period) held monthly payment costs in check even as home prices soared. This masked structural deterioration in the underlying price-to-income relationship. The rate normalization of 2022–2023 exposed the true depth of the affordability problem.

Methodology

Data Sources

  • U.S. Census Bureau — Median Home Sale Price
  • U.S. Census Bureau — Median Household Income
  • BLS CPI-U — Consumer Price Index (All Urban)
  • FRED — 30-Year Fixed Mortgage Rate

Transformations Applied

  • All series annualized to calendar year
  • Base-year indexing (1985 = 100) for comparability
  • CPI deflation for real home price construction
  • Log transformation for growth rate analysis

Constructed Metrics

  • Price-to-Income Ratio (P/I)
  • Real Home Price Index (CPI-adjusted)
  • Affordability Gap Index (Home Price Index ÷ Income Index × 100)
  • Rate Pressure Index (Mortgage Rate × P/I Ratio)

Limitations & Notes

  • Median home price reflects transactions, not stock
  • Income measure is pre-tax household median
  • Geographic variation not captured in national series
  • 2025 row excluded due to incomplete annual data
