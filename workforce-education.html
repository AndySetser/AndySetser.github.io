How much is a college degree worth in the U.S. labor market, and does that premium hold after controlling for experience, gender, and race? This analysis estimates a standard Mincer earnings equation on Current Population Survey microdata spanning 2010 to 2023, using log annual wages as the outcome variable and education, experience, and demographic controls as predictors.

The sample is restricted to full-time workers (35+ hours per week) aged 25–64 with positive wage income, yielding 263,863 observations across five survey years. Three nested OLS models are estimated, progressively adding controls to test the stability of the education coefficients.

"Education coefficients are stable across all three model specifications; the wage premium is not an artifact of omitted experience or demographic controls. It is a persistent feature of the U.S. wage structure."

Key Findings

1. The Education Premium Is Large and Stable

Relative to workers with less than a high school diploma, Bachelor's degree holders earn approximately 93% more in log-wage terms in the fully controlled model. Graduate degree holders earn 124% more. These coefficients shift by only 1–2 percentage points when adding experience, gender, and race controls — indicating that the education premium is not driven by selection on observable characteristics.

Even a high school diploma carries a meaningful premium: a 34.4% log-wage advantage over those without one, robust across all three specifications.

2. The Gender Gap Persists After Full Controls

The female coefficient is −0.325 in Model 3, implying a 32.5% unexplained wage gap after controlling for education, experience, race, and year. This is consistent with the broader labor economics literature documenting persistent wage penalties for women that are not explained by human capital differences. Factors like occupational sorting, hours flexibility, and discrimination are candidate explanations not captured here.

3. Experience Has a Concave Profile

The experience coefficient is positive (0.033) and the squared term is negative (−0.001), replicating Mincer's original finding of a concave experience-earnings profile. Wages rise with experience but at a diminishing rate, with peak earnings implied around 25–28 years of experience under these estimates.

4. The Racial Wage Gap Is Significant for Black Workers

Black workers face a statistically significant wage penalty of approximately 14.4% relative to White workers with identical education, experience, and year controls. The coefficient for Asian workers is not statistically significant, suggesting no measurable gap after these controls. These are descriptive OLS estimates — they do not identify discrimination, but they document unexplained dispersion that warrants attention.

5. Real Wages Grew Substantially After 2019

Year dummy coefficients show strong real wage growth across the sample period: +4.9% by 2015, +17.0% by 2019, +21.6% by 2021, and +33.6% by 2023 relative to the 2010 reference year. The acceleration post-2019 is consistent with the tight pandemic-era labor market and is one of the largest wage growth periods in the dataset.

R Code

Fully reproducible analysis. Data from IPUMS CPS; register and extract at ipums.org/cps.

01 — Data Preparation
library(tidyverse)
library(modelsummary)
library(scales)
library(viridis)
 
cps <- read_csv("cps_00001.csv")
 
cps_clean <- cps |>
  filter(AGE >= 25, AGE <= 64) |>
  filter(INCWAGE > 0, INCWAGE < 99999999) |>
  filter(UHRSWORKT >= 35, UHRSWORKT < 997) |>
  mutate(
    education = case_when(
      EDUC < 73                    ~ "1. Less than High School",
      EDUC == 73                   ~ "2. High School Diploma",
      EDUC %in% c(80,81,90,91,92) ~ "3. Some College",
      EDUC == 111                  ~ "4. Bachelor's Degree",
      EDUC >= 123                  ~ "5. Graduate Degree"
    ),
    gender          = if_else(SEX == 1, "Male", "Female"),
    years_of_school = case_when(
      EDUC < 73  ~ 10, EDUC == 73 ~ 12,
      EDUC %in% c(80,81,90,91,92) ~ 14,
      EDUC == 111 ~ 16, EDUC >= 123 ~ 18
    ),
    experience    = AGE - years_of_school - 6,
    experience_sq = experience^2,
    log_wage      = log(INCWAGE),
    female        = if_else(SEX == 2, 1, 0),
    black         = if_else(RACE == 200, 1, 0),
    asian         = if_else(RACE == 651, 1, 0),
    year_2015     = if_else(YEAR == 2015, 1, 0),
    year_2019     = if_else(YEAR == 2019, 1, 0),
    year_2021     = if_else(YEAR == 2021, 1, 0),
    year_2023     = if_else(YEAR == 2023, 1, 0)
  ) |>
  filter(!is.na(education), experience >= 0)
02 — OLS Models
# Model 1: Education only
model1 <- lm(log_wage ~ education, data = cps_clean)
 
# Model 2: Add experience and gender
model2 <- lm(log_wage ~ education + experience + experience_sq + female,
             data = cps_clean)
 
# Model 3: Full controls
model3 <- lm(log_wage ~ education + experience + experience_sq +
               female + black + asian +
               year_2015 + year_2019 + year_2021 + year_2023,
             data = cps_clean)
 
modelsummary(
  list("Model 1" = model1, "Model 2" = model2, "Model 3" = model3),
  stars = TRUE, fmt = 3
)
03 — Education Wage Premium Chart
# Predict wages for reference worker at each education level
ref_worker <- data.frame(
  education     = c("1. Less than High School", "2. High School Diploma",
                    "3. Some College", "4. Bachelor's Degree", "5. Graduate Degree"),
  experience    = 10, experience_sq = 100,
  female        = 0, black = 0, asian = 0,
  year_2015     = 0, year_2019 = 1, year_2021 = 0, year_2023 = 0
)
 
ref_worker$predicted_wage <- exp(predict(model3, newdata = ref_worker))
 
ref_worker |>
  ggplot(aes(x = reorder(edu_label, predicted_wage), y = predicted_wage,
             fill = edu_label)) +
  geom_col(width = 0.6, show.legend = FALSE) +
  scale_fill_viridis_d(option = "plasma") +
  coord_flip() +
  scale_y_continuous(labels = dollar_format()) +
  labs(title = "The Education Wage Premium",
       caption = "Source: IPUMS CPS 2010–2023 | OLS Mincer earnings equation") +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 14)

Methodology & Variables

IPUMS CPS extract variables and analytical decisions.

CPS Variables Used

  • YEAR — Survey year
  • AGE — Age (filtered: 25–64)
  • SEX — Gender (binary: Male/Female)
  • RACE — Race/ethnicity
  • EDUC — Educational attainment
  • INCWAGE — Annual wage income (outcome)
  • UHRSWORKT — Usual weekly hours (filter: ≥ 35)
  • WTFINL — Survey weight (included)

Sample Restrictions

  • Ages 25–64 (prime working age)
  • Full-time workers: UHRSWORKT ≥ 35
  • Positive wage income: INCWAGE > 0
  • Excludes top-coded wages (INCWAGE = 99,999,999)
  • Drops observations with missing education
  • Drops implied negative experience (experience ≥ 0)
  • Survey years: 2010, 2015, 2019, 2021, 2023

Constructed Variables

  • log_wage = log(INCWAGE) — outcome
  • years_of_school — mapped from EDUC codes
  • experience = AGE − years_of_school − 6
  • experience_sq = experience² (non-linearity)
  • Binary dummies: female, black, asian
  • Year dummies (2010 = reference)

Limitations

  • Education is likely endogenous (ability selection)
  • OLS estimates causal returns with upward bias
  • IV approach (e.g. Card 1995) needed for causality
  • Annual wages conflate wage rates and hours worked
  • Race categories simplified from full IPUMS taxonomy
  • Geographic variation not controlled
