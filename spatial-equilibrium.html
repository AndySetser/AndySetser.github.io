Spatial Equilibrium

Unsure of my long term location and the type of place that I want to end up, I began thinking about the economics behind how a location is landed on. I had the idea for a hedonic pricing tool that pulls live Census Bureau and BEA data for U.S. counties and towns. Through the help of a connection that understood Html coding, I managed a live model that can run a hedonic regression across 3,055 counties, and can interpret that data in equations that are useful for understanding the cost of a location.

I grew up in Wichita, Kansas, and have lived in Alamosa, Colorado since 2019. I have complained about both places equally. But, recently have acknowledged that these complaints are useful, because they say something about how we land on a place and why a framework for it exist.

Theory

Hedonic Pricing

Sherwin Rosen, in 1974, argued that the goods you buy, whether a car or a house, are a bundle of characteristics, each with its own cost factored into the listing price.1 A house is not just a house. It is square footage, a school district, a commute, a climate, a view, all priced separately even though only one number shows face.

Jennifer Roback (1982) took that idea and applied it to entire places.2 A location, in her framework, is a bundle of wages, amenities, and taxes. This settles into what economists call spatial equilibrium: a condition where the advantage of a desirable place weakens until the resident is indifferent between staying or leaving. Enough people compete for places worth living that the benefits eventually get outbid. My version of a perfect place has good running trails, coffee, breweries, and fewer mosquitoes. But the market already knows these are desirable traits, and will charge me for them if I ever decide to move toward them.

Labour and housing markets

Roback's key insight is that amenities show up in two separate markets at the same time. The labor market and the housing market, and they can push in opposite directions. If a place is pleasant to live in, workers should be willing to accept lower wages because the amenity is part of their compensation. At the same time, everyone else wants to live there too, which bids up rent. A true consumption amenity should show up as lower wages and higher rents together.

A production amenity, something that makes workers more productive rather than happier, like proximity to other skilled workers, tends to raise both wages and rents at once. You can tell the difference between "this place makes you productive" and "this place makes you happy" by watching which direction wages move relative to rent. Blomquist, Berger, and Hoehn (1988) used this two-equation approach to rank the dollar value of quality of life across hundreds of U.S. cities.3

Compensating Differentials

Adam Smith (1776) noticed that unpleasant or dangerous work commands a wage premium, what economists now call a compensating differential.4 Mining pays more than an equally skilled desk job because someone has to be paid to accept the risk. The same relationship applies to locations, only in reverse. A location with disadvantages, hard winters, isolation, a thin labor market, should in theory require higher pay to keep people there. In practice, rural places like the one I live in often pay less, which is one of the harder questions in regional economics. Part of the answer is that low wages get offset by an even lower cost of living. Part of it is that people are not entirely economically rational about where they stay. Family and inertia absorb some of the wage gap that theory would predict.

Local Government

Charles Tiebout (1956) added an additional layer; people do not only sort by wages and rent, they also sort by local government.5 Different jurisdictions offer different bundles of taxes, schools, parks, and services, and people move toward the bundle that suits them. A good school district is a policy choice that the housing market prices the same way it prices a mountain view.

What the Regression Misses

The model is quite simple and misses many other factors. It leaves out zoning rules, school quality, crime rates, and broadband access. It also leaves out proximity to family, which hedonic models always handle poorly because it resists quantification. For most people, family outbids every quality-of-life index and cost-of-living calculator out there. Social capital is real, just hard to price. Relocating and rebuilding it somewhere new takes longer and costs more than any regression can account for.

Methodology

Model

  • OLS regression of median home value on median household income and USDA natural amenity score
  • Fit across all 3,055 U.S. counties with data for both variables
  • Residual shows how far each county's home value differs from the national pattern
  • Goes back to income-only model when amenity score is missing

Data Sources

  • ACS 5-year estimates, vintage 2020–2024 (Census Bureau API)
  • BEA Regional Data, CAINC1, per-capita personal income (BEA API)
  • USDA ERS Natural Amenities Scale, county level only
  • Rent burden is from median rent divided by renter household income (B25119)

What Is Included

  • Population, poverty rate, vacancy rate
  • Median home value, median gross rent
  • Median household income (all households and renter households)
  • Home price-to-income ratio, rent burden, natural amenity score, per-capita personal income

Limitations

  • ACS 5-year estimates lag 2 to 7 years behind real time
  • Model lacks zoning, school quality, crime, broadband access
  • City and town mode covers only places reported in Census Bureau
  • Small geographies carry wider margins of error
